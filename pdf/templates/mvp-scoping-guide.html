What an MVP is for

A minimum viable product is the smallest thing you can put in front of real users to learn whether your riskiest assumption is true. It is a learning tool first and a product second.

Most MVPs run late for the same reason: the scope grows to cover every user, every edge case and every feature a competitor has. This guide works the other way. You start from the one thing you most need to learn, and keep only what it takes to learn it.

Signs your scope is too big

  • ☐ It serves more than one type of user equally (for example buyers and sellers and admins, all fully featured).
  • ☐ The feature list has more than about ten "must have" items.
  • ☐ You cannot say in one sentence what you will learn from launch.
  • ☐ It includes features "for later" that are being built now "because it's easier".
  • ☐ Nobody outside the team has used a prototype or a manual version yet.

If you ticked two or more, the steps that follow will save you time and money.

Example (illustrative)

A booking tool for independent physiotherapists: (1) patient opens the clinic's link, (2) picks a slot, (3) enters details and pays a deposit, (4) gets a confirmation, (5) clinic sees the booking on its calendar, (6) both get a reminder. Everything else (reporting, multi-clinic support, packages) waits.

Moments that must feel right

Mark the one or two steps where users decide whether the product is worth it. Spend design time there first.

Questions that cut scope

  • ☐ Could an admin do this by hand for the first 50 users?
  • ☐ Could an existing tool (Stripe Checkout, a scheduling tool, a form builder, email) do it instead of custom code?
  • ☐ Does it serve the primary user, or someone secondary?
  • ☐ Would the learning goal from step 2 change if we left it out?
  • ☐ Is it needed on day one, or once there are paying customers?

Step 6

How to build it

Pick the approach that tests your assumption fastest without blocking what comes next. There is no single right answer; the trade-offs are below.

ApproachGood whenWatch out for
No-code
Website builders, form and automation tools, no-code app builders		You are testing demand or a workflow; logic is simple; speed matters most.Platform limits, per-user pricing as you grow, hard migrations later, data residency.
Low-code
Backend-as-a-service plus a custom front end		You need a custom experience but standard back-end pieces (auth, database, storage).Vendor lock-in for data and auth; pricing at scale.
Custom codeThe product's value is in the technology itself, you need deep integrations, or you are in a regulated space.Higher upfront cost and time; you need a team to maintain it.
HybridCustom where it matters (the core journey), off-the-shelf for the rest (payments, email, analytics, admin).Too many tools stitched together without an owner.

Decide

  • ☐ What must the MVP prove, and can a no-code version prove it?
  • ☐ Who owns the code, designs and accounts at the end? (Put it in the contract.)
  • ☐ What happens if the MVP works: can it carry the first paying customers, or is a rebuild planned?
  • ☐ Who maintains it after launch, and how are bugs handled?

Launch plan

  • ☐ The first 10 to 50 users are named people you can talk to, not anonymous traffic.
  • ☐ Analytics events for the core journey are firing and tested.
  • ☐ A feedback channel is visible in the product (an email, a chat, a short form).
  • ☐ A weekly review is booked for the first eight weeks after launch.
  • ☐ The "Won't have" list from step 4 is ready for the next round of decisions.
