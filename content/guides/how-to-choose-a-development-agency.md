To choose a development agency, judge how it works as much as what it charges: ask to try products it has shipped, look for a discovery phase before a fixed build quote, expect weekly demos and named people on your project, and get a contract that transfers ownership of the code, designs and accounts to you once you've paid. Walk away from agencies that quote a large build without asking questions, or that won't put ownership in writing.

This guide gives you the questions to ask, the red flags to watch for, and the contract terms that protect you, even if you can't read a line of code.

:::takeaways
- You can't easily judge code quality, but you **can judge process, communication and contracts**.
- Ask to **use** something the agency has built, not just see screenshots.
- A good agency asks you hard questions and often suggests a **paid discovery phase** before quoting a full build.
- Get **ownership of code, designs, domains and accounts** in writing. It isn't automatic everywhere.
:::

## 12 questions to ask a development agency

### About their work

1. **"Can I use something you've built that's similar to my project?"** Live products you can sign up for, download or click through tell you more than a portfolio of screenshots. Our own [Studios work](studios:/work/) links to the live products where they're public.
2. **"What exactly did you do on that project?"** Design only, build only, or everything? Agencies sometimes show work where they did one small part.
3. **"Can I speak to a past client?"** Ask that client how the agency handled problems and changes, not just whether they liked the result.

### About their process

4. **"What happens in the first two weeks?"** Listen for discovery: understanding users, defining the core task, prototyping. Be wary of "we'll start coding right away."
5. **"How will I see progress?"** Weekly demos of working software are the standard to expect. Status reports alone aren't enough.
6. **"How do you handle changes to scope?"** There should be a clear, written process: what a change costs, and how it affects the timeline.
7. **"How do you test before launch?"** Ask what's tested, on which devices, and who does it.

### About the people

8. **"Who will actually work on my project?"** Get names and roles. Ask whether any work is subcontracted, and to whom.
9. **"Who is my day-to-day contact, and how fast do they reply?"** Communication problems cause more failed projects than technical ones.

### About the business side

10. **"Who owns the code, designs and accounts, and when?"** The answer should be you, on payment, in writing. More on this below.
11. **"What happens after launch?"** Ask about the warranty period for bugs, support options, maintenance costs and documentation.
12. **"If we stop working together, how do I take everything with me?"** A confident agency has a clear handover answer.

:::callout tip "Listen to their questions, too"
The quality of an agency's questions is one of the best signals you'll get. A team that asks about your users, your riskiest assumption and your budget before quoting is thinking about your outcome. A team that only asks about features is thinking about the build.
:::

## Red flags that should make you walk away

:::checklist "Red flags"
- A fixed price for a large build with no questions or discovery
- Can't show anything live that you can try
- Won't name the people who will work on your project
- Vague about who owns the code, or says it stays with them
- Hosting, domain or app store accounts registered in their name, not yours
- No written process for scope changes
- Large upfront payment with no milestones
- Promises that sound too good: "full app in two weeks," "no bugs guaranteed"
- Pushes more features than your problem needs
- Slow or unclear communication before you've even signed
:::

One red flag isn't always fatal; ask about it. Several together usually predict a difficult project.

:::myth-fact
Myth: The lowest quote saves the most money.
Fact: Quotes often cover different scope. A low quote may leave out design, testing or handover, and a rebuild costs far more than the difference. Compare what's included line by line.
Myth: If I pay for the software, I automatically own it.
Fact: Not necessarily. In many places, the creator keeps copyright unless a written agreement transfers it. Get ownership terms in the contract.
Myth: A bigger agency is always safer.
Fact: Size matters less than fit. What counts is relevant experience, named senior people on your project, and a process you can see working every week.
:::

## Who owns the code? Contract terms to get in writing

This is the part business owners most often skip, and regret later.

**Copyright doesn't always follow payment.** Rules differ by country. In the United States, for example, the US Copyright Office explains that work made by an independent contractor only counts as a "work made for hire" in specific circumstances with a signed written agreement ([Circular 30](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf)). Otherwise, the creator generally keeps the copyright unless it's assigned to you in writing. Other countries have their own rules. This isn't legal advice: have a lawyer where you operate check the contract.

Make sure the contract covers:

| Term | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Intellectual property | Ownership of code, designs and content transfers to you on payment |
| Third-party and open-source code | A list of libraries and licenses used, and confirmation they allow your use |
| Accounts and access | Domains, hosting, app store and cloud accounts in your company's name, with admin access for you |
| Source code access | Code kept in a repository you own or can access at any time |
| Scope and changes | What's included, and how changes are priced and approved |
| Milestones and payment | Payments tied to delivered, demonstrated milestones |
| Warranty and support | How long bugs are fixed for free after launch, and support options after that |
| Confidentiality | Your idea, data and business information are protected |
| Data protection | How personal data is handled during development and testing |
| Termination and handover | What you receive, and in what form, if the project ends early |

:::quote "Joanna Okedara-Kalu" "Founder & CEO, TechDella"
My advice to every founder is to ask about ownership and handover in the first call, not the last. It tells you a lot about a team. If an agency gets uncomfortable when you ask how you'd leave them, that's your answer. The partners worth keeping are the ones who make it easy to go, and earn the reason you stay.
:::

## A simple selection process

:::steps "Choosing an agency in four weeks"
1. Write your brief | One or two pages: problem, users, core task, must-haves, budget range, deadline.
2. Shortlist three agencies | Pick teams with relevant, live work you can try.
3. Run the same call with each | Use the 12 questions above and take notes in the comparison table.
4. Check references | Speak to at least one past client per agency about how problems were handled.
5. Review the contract | Check ownership, accounts, milestones and handover, ideally with a lawyer.
6. Start small | Begin with discovery or a prototype. It's the cheapest way to test the relationship.
:::

:::faq "FAQ"
### What should I ask a software development agency before hiring?
Ask to try similar work they've shipped, what exactly they did on it, what happens in the first two weeks, how you'll see progress, how scope changes are handled, who will work on your project, who owns the code and accounts, and what happens after launch and at handover.
### What are red flags when hiring a development agency?
A big fixed quote with no questions, no live work you can try, unnamed team members, vague code ownership, accounts registered in the agency's name, a large upfront payment without milestones, and promises such as a complete app in two weeks. Several together usually predict a difficult project.
### Do I own the code an agency writes for me?
Only if your contract says so. In many countries, including the United States, the creator generally keeps copyright unless there's a written agreement transferring it. Make sure the contract assigns code, designs and content to you on payment, and have a lawyer review it.
### Should I start with a small project before a full build?
Yes, where you can. A paid discovery phase or prototype tests how the agency communicates, plans and delivers, and gives you a tested scope for the build. If the relationship doesn't work, you've spent far less and still have something useful.
:::

You don't need to read code to choose a good development partner. Judge the work you can try, the questions they ask, the process you'll see every week, and the contract that decides what you own.
