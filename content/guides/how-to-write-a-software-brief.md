A software brief is a short document that tells a development team what problem you want solved, for whom, and what the first version must do, so they can quote accurately and build the right thing. The best briefs are one or two pages, written in plain language, and focus on users and outcomes rather than technology. You don't need to be technical to write one; you need to be clear.

This guide walks through the nine parts of a strong brief, gives you a template to copy, and lists the mistakes that lead to bad quotes.

:::takeaways
- Describe the **problem and the people**, not the technology. Leave the "how" to the team.
- Name **one core task** the first version must handle well. Everything else goes on a "later" list.
- Always include a **budget range and a deadline**. Without them, quotes can't be compared.
- A one-page brief that's clear beats a 40-page spec that's vague.
:::

## Why the brief matters more than you think

Two teams can read the same unclear brief and quote very different prices, because each fills the gaps with its own assumptions. One imagines a simple web app; the other imagines iOS and Android apps with an admin dashboard. Neither is wrong. The brief just didn't say.

A clear brief does three things for you:

- **Comparable quotes.** Every team prices the same scope.
- **Faster start.** Less time in discovery calls clarifying basics.
- **Fewer surprises.** Fewer "that wasn't included" conversations halfway through.

## The nine parts of a good software brief

### 1. One-line summary

What is this, in a sentence? "A booking app that lets clients of small beauty salons book and pay a deposit from their phone." If you can't write this line yet, you're not ready for a brief; start with [product validation](journal:/product-validation-for-startups/).

### 2. The problem

What's going wrong today, and what does it cost? Describe the current workaround: spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads, paper, a tool that doesn't fit. Concrete beats abstract: "Our team spends every Monday matching payments to invoices by hand" is more useful than "we need better finance processes."

### 3. Who it's for

Describe the main user. If there are several kinds (customers, staff, admins), list them and say which one matters most for the first version. Include practical details that change the build: what devices they use, how comfortable they are with technology, what languages they read, whether their internet connection is reliable.

### 4. The core task

The single most important thing a user must be able to do, start to finish. "A salon client finds a free slot, books it and pays a deposit." Teams build better products when they know which flow must be excellent.

### 5. Must-haves, and a "later" list

List the features the first version can't launch without. Then write a second list of things you want eventually but not now. The "later" list is just as important: it shows the team where the edges are. Our guide on [how to develop an MVP](journal:/how-to-develop-an-mvp-for-startup/) explains how to cut scope.

### 6. What exists already

Brand guidelines, an existing website or app, designs, a prototype (including a vibe-coded one), data you'll need to import, tools you already pay for. Also say which systems it must connect to: payment providers, accounting software, a CRM, email tools.

### 7. Constraints

Anything that limits choices: where data must be stored, industry rules you must follow, accessibility needs, a technology your team already uses, a launch date tied to an event.

### 8. Budget range and timeline

Give a range, even a rough one. Teams don't use it to "spend it all"; they use it to suggest what's realistic. A budget range lets them say "for that, we'd recommend starting with a prototype" instead of quoting something you can't afford. Name your deadline and why it matters.

### 9. How you'll measure success

What will tell you, three months after launch, that this worked? "Half of bookings come through the app instead of WhatsApp." "Payment matching takes an hour a week instead of a day." This keeps every decision during the build pointed at a result.

:::callout warning "Don't specify the technology unless you must"
Writing "build it in React with a Node backend" when you're not technical often backfires: you may rule out a simpler, cheaper option. Describe the outcome you need and any real constraint (for example, "my team already maintains a WordPress site"), then ask the team to recommend the technology and explain why.
:::

## A software brief template you can copy

| Section | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | One sentence | Booking and deposit app for small beauty salons |
| Problem | Today's workaround and its cost | Bookings arrive by WhatsApp; no-shows cost each salon several slots a week |
| Users | Main user first, then others | Salon clients (phone, mixed tech comfort); salon owner; staff |
| Core task | One flow, start to finish | Client finds slot, books, pays deposit, gets reminder |
| Must-haves | Launch list | Booking calendar, deposit payment, SMS or WhatsApp reminder, owner's day view |
| Later | Not in version one | Loyalty points, product sales, multi-branch support |
| Existing assets | Brand, data, tools | Logo and colors; client list in a spreadsheet; uses a local payment provider |
| Constraints | Rules and limits | Customer data stays in-country; must work on low-end Android phones |
| Budget and timeline | Range and date | A range you're comfortable with; live before the holiday season |
| Success | What changes in 3 months | Half of bookings through the app; fewer no-shows |

The example above is illustrative, not a client project. Replace each line with your own details.

:::myth-fact
Myth: A longer, more detailed spec gets you a better product.
Fact: Long specs often lock in guesses before anyone has tested them. A short brief with a clear core task and a "later" list leaves room for discovery to improve the plan.
Myth: If I share my budget, the agency will just charge me all of it.
Fact: A budget range lets a good team recommend the right-sized first version. Without it, you'll get quotes for very different products that you can't compare.
:::

## Common brief mistakes

:::checklist "Check your brief for these"
- It describes features but never says who uses them or why
- Every feature is marked "must-have"
- There's no budget range or deadline
- It names a technology without a reason
- Success is "a great app" instead of a measurable change
- It copies a competitor's feature list
- It leaves out existing systems the product must connect to
- Nobody else has read it; ask a colleague to explain it back to you
:::

:::faq "FAQ"
### How long should a software brief be?
One or two pages is enough for most first versions. Cover the problem, users, core task, must-haves, a "later" list, existing assets, constraints, budget range, timeline and success measure. Detail can come later in discovery, once the team has tested the core flow with a prototype.
### Should I include a budget in my brief?
Yes. A range, even a rough one, lets teams recommend what's realistic and makes their quotes comparable. Without it, one team may quote a simple prototype and another a full product with apps and dashboards, and you can't compare them.
### Do I need to know the technology to write a brief?
No. Describe the problem, the people, the core task and any real constraints. Let the team recommend the technology and explain why. Only specify technology if you have a genuine reason, such as an existing system your team already maintains.
### What is the difference between a brief and a specification?
A brief explains the problem, users and goals so a team can propose and quote. A specification describes exactly how the product will work, screen by screen. The brief comes first; the specification usually comes out of discovery and design, after testing.
:::

## The bottom line

You don't need technical language to brief a development team well. Explain the problem, the people and the one task that matters, be honest about budget and deadline, and say what you're unsure of.
