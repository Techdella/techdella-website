UX design (user experience design) is the work of deciding how your product should work for the people who use it: what they're trying to do, the steps they take, and what might confuse or stop them. UI design is how those screens look. For a business owner, good UX means fewer abandoned signups, fewer support calls and less money spent rebuilding features nobody could use.

This guide explains UX in plain terms, what a UX design sprint gives you, and how to judge a design when you don't have a design background.

:::takeaways
- **UX is how it works; UI is how it looks.** You need both, but UX comes first.
- A UX sprint gives you **user flows, wireframes and a clickable prototype**, tested before anyone writes code.
- Changing a design on screen is far cheaper than changing built software. That's the business case for UX.
- Judge a design by **whether users can finish the main task**, not by personal taste.
:::

## UX vs UI: the simple difference

Think about a restaurant. UX is the layout: where the door is, how you find a table, how you order and pay. UI is the decor: the colors, the menu typeface, the lighting. A beautiful restaurant with a confusing ordering system still loses customers.

| | UX (user experience) | UI (user interface) |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | Can people do what they came to do? | Does it look clear, consistent and on-brand? |
| Main outputs | Research notes, user flows, wireframes, prototypes | Visual screens, colors, type, icons, components |
| When it happens | First | After the flows work |
| How you test it | Watch users try to complete tasks | Check readability, consistency and brand fit |
| What goes wrong without it | People get lost, give up or call support | The product looks untrustworthy or messy |

Most agencies, including ours, offer them together as UI/UX design. When you're reviewing work, it helps to know which question you're answering at each stage.

## Why UX matters to the bottom line

Design isn't only about looks. McKinsey studied 300 publicly listed companies over five years and found that the ones that scored highest on design practices grew faster than their industry peers.

:::stats "The business case for design" highlight=0
32 points | higher revenue growth over five years for top-quartile design performers than their industry peers | McKinsey, The business value of design, 2018 | https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-design/our-insights/the-business-value-of-design
5 users | in a round of testing typically reveal most of a design's usability problems, so small, repeated tests work well | Nielsen Norman Group, 2000 | https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/
:::

The McKinsey finding is a correlation across large companies, not a promise for your product. The practical point for a small business is simpler: problems found on a prototype cost hours to fix. The same problems found after launch cost weeks of development and lost customers.

## What a UX design sprint gives you

A UX sprint is a short, fixed period (usually two to three weeks) focused on getting the core of your product right before it's built. At Techdella Studios a UI/UX Design Sprint starts from {{price:uiux_sprint}} and runs 2 to 3 weeks with 2 revision rounds. Whoever you work with, you should come out with these:

:::steps "What you should get from a UX sprint"
1. A clear goal and user | One sentence on who the product is for and the main job they need done.
2. User flows | Simple diagrams of the steps a user takes, such as "sign up, add a product, share the store link."
3. Wireframes | Plain black-and-white layouts showing what goes on each screen, without colors distracting the discussion.
4. A clickable prototype | Screens linked together so you can tap through them on a phone or laptop as if the product were real.
5. Test findings | Notes from real users trying the prototype: where they hesitated, what they misread, what they asked.
6. A prioritized list of changes | What to fix before build, what can wait, and what to drop.
:::

If a proposal says "UX" but only shows final, colored screens, ask where the flows, wireframes and testing are. Skipping them is how products end up looking good and working badly.

:::callout tip "Bring your own evidence"
Before the sprint, collect what you already know: customer questions, complaints, sales objections, screenshots of tools your customers use today. A designer can't interview your customers' history; you can share it.
:::

## How to judge a design without a design background

Business owners are often asked "what do you think?" about screens, and feel they can only answer with taste. Use these five questions instead. They work for apps, websites and dashboards.

1. **Can I tell what this screen is for in five seconds?** If you have to think, so will your customers.
2. **Is the main action obvious?** There should be one clear next step on each screen, usually a single prominent button.
3. **Does it use my customers' words?** Labels should match how customers talk, not internal jargon. "Send invoice," not "Initiate billing workflow."
4. **What happens when something goes wrong?** Ask to see the error, empty and loading states. A form with no helpful error message loses people.
5. **Has a real user tried it?** Opinions in a meeting, including yours, are weaker evidence than watching one target customer use it.

When you give feedback, describe the problem, not the solution. "I don't know where to tap to pay" helps a designer more than "make the button red."

:::myth-fact
Myth: UX is just making things look nice.
Fact: Looks are UI. UX decides whether people can complete what they came to do. A plain screen that works beats a beautiful one that confuses.
Myth: I know my customers, so we can skip testing.
Fact: You know the problem well, which makes it harder to see the product as a newcomer does. Watching even a handful of target users reveals issues insiders miss.
Myth: UX design slows the project down.
Fact: It moves the slow part earlier, to where changes are cheap. Fixing a flow on a prototype takes hours; fixing it in built software takes far longer.
:::

## What good UX looked like on real builds

Our Studios team delivered end-to-end UI/UX for [Paycape](studios:/work/paycape-website/), [Shopinbos](studios:/work/shopinbos-store-builder/) and [startbuddi](studios:/work/startbuddi-website/). The common thread was the same each time: start from the jobs the user does most often and make those flows effortless before adding anything else. For Paycape, that meant the monthly jobs of a Nigerian business owner (payroll, invoicing, tax). For Shopinbos, it meant getting a first-time seller from sign-up to a working store quickly. For startbuddi, it meant bookings, payments and client notes in one place for busy service businesses.

:::quote "Joanna Okedara-Kalu" "Founder & CEO, TechDella"
The most useful thing an owner can do in a design review is stop asking "do I like it?" and start asking "could my least technical customer finish this on a cheap phone with bad signal?" That question changes the conversation. Suddenly we're talking about fewer steps, bigger buttons and clearer words, which is exactly where the money is.
:::

## How UX connects to growth after launch

UX doesn't stop at launch. Once real users arrive, analytics shows where they drop off, and that's where design work pays again. Improving those steps is called [conversion rate optimization](marketing:/glossary/conversion-rate-optimization/), and testing two versions against each other is [A/B testing](marketing:/glossary/a-b-testing/). Both start from the same habit a UX sprint builds: watch what users do, then change the design.

## When to invest in UX

UX work is most valuable:

- **Before an MVP build.** Test the core flow first. Our guide to [how to develop an MVP](journal:/how-to-develop-an-mvp-for-startup/) explains where it fits.
- **When signups or sales drop off at the same step.** That's a flow problem, not a traffic problem.
- **When support keeps answering the same question.** Repeated questions usually point to a confusing screen.
- **Before a redesign.** Find out what's actually broken before you change everything.

If you haven't yet confirmed people want your product at all, start with [product validation](journal:/product-validation-for-startups/). UX makes a wanted product easy to use; it can't create demand that isn't there.

:::faq "FAQ"
### What does a UX designer do for a business?
A UX designer works out how your product should work for its users. They research what users need, map the steps to complete key tasks, sketch wireframes, build a clickable prototype and test it with real people. The result is a product that's easier to use, with problems fixed before development.
### What is the difference between UX and UI design?
UX is how a product works: the flow, the steps and whether users can finish their task. UI is how it looks: colors, typography, icons and layout details. UX comes first, then UI makes the working flow clear and on-brand. Most studios deliver both together.
### How long does a UX design sprint take?
Usually two to three weeks for the core flows of a product. At Techdella Studios, a UI/UX Design Sprint runs 2 to 3 weeks with 2 revision rounds and starts from {{price:uiux_sprint}}. Larger products with many user types take longer or run in several sprints.
### How do I give feedback on a design?
Describe the problem you see rather than the fix you want, and tie it to a user task. "I can't tell how to pay" is more useful than "move this button." Ask to see error and empty states, and ask whether real users have tried it.
:::
