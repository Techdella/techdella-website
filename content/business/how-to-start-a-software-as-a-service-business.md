To start a SaaS business, start with a problem, not with code. Find a specific type of customer with a painful, recurring problem, prove they'll pay to fix it, then build the smallest product that solves one job well. Charge a subscription from the start, and watch three numbers closely: monthly recurring revenue, churn, and how long it takes each customer to pay back what it cost to acquire them.

That sounds simple. The hard part is discipline: not building features nobody asked for, and not spending money on growth before customers stay.

:::takeaways
- Validate with money: pre-sales, paid pilots or letters of intent beat survey answers.
- Ship a narrow MVP that does one job better than the spreadsheet or tool it replaces.
- Subscription revenue only compounds if churn is low. Retention comes before growth.
- Running out of cash is the most common way startups die, usually after a product or market problem.
:::

## What makes a SaaS business different

A [SaaS business](/glossary/saas-business/) sells access to software over the internet, usually by monthly or annual subscription. Customers don't install or maintain anything. You host, update and support it.

That model has three consequences:

- **Revenue builds slowly, then compounds.** Each month's new customers add to last month's, as long as they stay.
- **Retention is everything.** A customer who cancels after two months may never repay the cost of winning them.
- **You're running a service, not shipping a product once.** Uptime, security, support and data protection are part of what customers pay for.

## Why SaaS startups fail

The most useful data on startup failure comes from post-mortems of companies that shut down. CB Insights' 2026 analysis of 431 venture-backed shutdowns found most cited more than one reason.

:::chart "Top reasons VC-backed startups shut down (% citing each, shutdowns since 2023)" unit=% max=100 highlight=1
Ran out of capital | 70
Poor product-market fit | 43
Bad timing or macro conditions | 29
Unsustainable unit economics | 19
source: CB Insights, The top 9 reasons startups fail, 2026 | https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/
:::

CB Insights notes that running out of capital is almost always the final cause, not the root problem. The root is often poor [product-market fit](/glossary/product-market-fit/) or unit economics that never worked. Both are things you can test early and cheaply.

## How to start a SaaS business, step by step

:::steps
1. Pick a customer and a job | "Clinic managers who schedule staff shifts in spreadsheets" is a starting point. "Small businesses" is not.
2. Interview 15–20 potential customers | Ask how they solve the problem today, what it costs them, and what they've tried. Don't pitch yet.
3. Validate with money | Offer early access at a discount for payment up front, or a paid pilot. Three to five paying users beat 200 survey responses.
4. Design the core workflow | Map the one job your product must do end to end. Test clickable designs with your pilot users before code.
5. Build a narrow MVP | Only what the core workflow needs, plus login, billing and basic security.
6. Onboard pilots by hand | Set up each early customer yourself. You'll learn what confuses people and what they value.
7. Set subscription pricing | Price on the value of the job done, with two or three plans at most.
8. Measure retention | Track active use and cancellations monthly. Fix the reasons people leave before spending on marketing.
9. Choose one growth channel | Search, partnerships, outbound or a marketplace. Commit for a quarter and measure the cost per customer.
:::

## SaaS unit economics: a worked example

These are illustrative numbers. Use them to build your own model.

- Price: **$50 a month** per customer.
- Gross margin after hosting, payments and support: **80%**, so each customer contributes $50 × 0.80 = **$40 a month**.
- [Customer acquisition cost](/glossary/customer-acquisition-cost/) (ads, content, sales time): **$400** per new customer.
- **CAC payback: $400 ÷ $40 = 10 months.**

Now add [churn](/glossary/customer-churn/). If 5% of customers cancel each month, the average customer stays about 1 ÷ 0.05 = **20 months**. Lifetime contribution: 20 × $40 = **$800**, or 2× what it cost to acquire them. Workable, but thin.

Cut churn to 2.5% a month and the average customer stays 40 months: 40 × $40 = **$1,600**, or 4× CAC. Same price, same marketing, double the value. That's why retention comes first.

:::callout key "The rule to remember"
If a customer doesn't stay long enough to repay what it cost to win them, more marketing makes the problem bigger, not smaller.
:::

## Getting your first 100 customers

- **Founder-led sales.** Talk to every early customer yourself. It's the fastest way to learn what to build next.
- **Search for problem-aware buyers.** People search for the job they need done, such as "staff rota software for clinics." Pages that answer those searches bring steady sign-ups over time; see how we approach [SEO](/services/seo/).
- **Integrations and partners.** Plug into the tools your customers already use, and list in their marketplaces.
- **Community.** Answer questions where your customers gather. Be useful first.

:::quote "Joanna Okedara-Kalu" "Founder & CEO, TechDella"
Search is slow to start, which is why founders give up on it too early. With Paycape, a fintech platform we worked with, the first result came 50 days in, and over a five-month engagement half of their conversions came from inbound. My advice is the same for any SaaS: write pages for the problem your buyers search, not for your feature list.
:::

:::myth-fact
Myth: If you build a great product, customers will come.
Fact: Distribution is part of the product. Plan how customers will find you before you build, and test that channel early.
Myth: More features make a SaaS product more valuable.
Fact: Early customers pay for one job done well. Extra features add maintenance and confusion before they add value.
Myth: Fundraising is the first milestone.
Fact: Paying customers who stay are the milestone. They make fundraising easier, or unnecessary.
:::

:::checklist "SaaS launch checklist"
- 15–20 customer interviews completed and written up
- At least three paying pilot customers
- Core workflow designed and tested with users
- MVP with login, billing, backups and basic security
- Terms, privacy policy and data processing terms in place
- Dashboard tracking MRR, churn and CAC
- One growth channel chosen for the next quarter
:::

:::faq
### How long does it take to start a SaaS business?
Validation can take a few weeks of interviews and pilot sales. Building the MVP depends on scope and team. The bigger timeline is reaching steady retention and growth, which usually takes many months of iteration after launch.
### Do I need to know how to code to start a SaaS company?
No, but you need a reliable way to build and maintain the product: a technical co-founder, a trusted development partner, or no-code tools for a first version. Non-technical founders should own customer discovery, sales and product decisions.
### How do SaaS businesses make money?
Through recurring subscriptions, usually monthly or annual, sometimes with usage-based charges or add-ons. Profit depends on gross margin, how much it costs to acquire customers, and how long they stay.
### What is a good churn rate for an early SaaS?
It varies by customer type and price. Small-business customers churn more than enterprise customers. Track your own monthly churn, find out why people cancel, and aim to bring it down every quarter.
### Can a Nigerian SaaS startup get the startup label?
Under the Nigeria Startup Act 2022, a limited liability company under 10 years old, focused on a digital technology product, with at least one-third Nigerian founder shareholding, is eligible. The label gives access to incentives under the Act. Check current procedures with the Act's secretariat before applying.
:::
