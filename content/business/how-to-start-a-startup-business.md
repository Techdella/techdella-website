To start a startup business, test your idea with the people you expect to pay before you spend serious money. Then write a one-page plan, register the company, build the smallest version that delivers your core promise, and launch it to a small group. Improve from what they do, not from what they say they'd do.

Most startup ideas never launch, and many that launch were never tested. This guide gives you a sequence that keeps risk low at every step, with notes for founders in Nigeria, the US and the UK.

:::takeaways
- Validate with behavior: pre-orders, deposits, sign-ups with a real cost, or pilots.
- A one-page plan beats a 50-page document. Focus it on your riskiest assumptions.
- Build a minimum viable product that tests the core promise, nothing more.
- Launch small and early. Your first users are a learning group, not a press event.
:::

## Startup or small business: which are you building?

Both are valid, and the steps overlap, but the goals differ:

- **A small business** serves a known market in a known way: a bakery, an accounting firm, a salon. The aim is a profitable, stable business.
- **A startup** is searching for a new, repeatable way to serve a market, often with technology, and aims to grow fast. It usually carries more uncertainty.

If you're not sure, ask: "Does my idea depend on something new working?" If yes, treat it as a startup and test that new thing first.

## How to start a startup business, step by step

:::steps "From idea to first users"
1. Write the problem in one sentence | Who has it, how often, and what it costs them. If you can't, you have a solution looking for a problem.
2. Talk to 20 potential customers | Ask about the last time they had the problem and what they did. Listen for workarounds and money spent.
3. Run a validation test with a real cost | A pre-order, deposit, waitlist with a paid reservation, or a paid pilot. Clicks are cheap; payment is evidence.
4. Write a one-page plan | Customer, problem, solution, how you'll reach customers, how you'll make money, and your three riskiest assumptions.
5. Register the company | So you can open a business account, sign contracts and protect your name.
6. Build the minimum viable product | The smallest thing that delivers the core promise. Manual work behind the scenes is fine at first.
7. Launch to a small group | Your validation list, early adopters and friendly customers. Watch what they use.
8. Measure and decide | Keep, change or drop features based on behavior. Repeat until people come back without being asked.
:::

For step 4, a quick [SWOT analysis](/glossary/swot-analysis/) can sit on the back of the one-page plan. ## Validation test: a worked example

Say you want to launch a meal-prep delivery service for busy professionals. Illustrative numbers:

- You set up a simple page offering a first week of meals at **30** (in your currency), with a 10 deposit to reserve.
- You share it with 400 people in your target group through WhatsApp groups and LinkedIn.
- **60** visit the page, and **18** pay a deposit. That's 18 ÷ 400 = **4.5%** of people reached, and 18 ÷ 60 = **30%** of visitors.
- Deposits collected: 18 × 10 = **180**. If 18 paying customers can be served profitably at 30 each, you've proven there's demand worth testing further.

If only two people paid, you learned that cheaply. Change the offer, audience or price and run it again. This is the core of [bootstrapping](/glossary/bootstrapping/): let customers fund the learning.

| | Nigeria | United States | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|---|

| Cost | Check current CAC fees on the portal | Usually under $300 total, varies by state | £100 online, usually registered within 24 hours |

## Common mistakes first-time founders make

- **Building in secret.** Months of building without customer contact is the most expensive way to learn.
- **Asking friends if they like the idea.** They'll say yes. Ask strangers to pay instead.
- **Over-building the MVP.** If you're not slightly embarrassed by version one, it probably took too long.
- **No co-founder agreement.** Write down equity, roles, vesting and what happens if someone leaves, before money or customers arrive.
- **Mixing personal and business money.** A separate account makes taxes, fundraising and sanity easier.

:::myth-fact
Myth: You need funding before you can start a startup.
Fact: Many startups validate and launch with savings and early customer revenue. Evidence of demand also makes raising money easier later.
Myth: A detailed business plan proves your idea will work.
Fact: Only customers prove it. A short plan helps you pick what to test; the tests give the answers.
Myth: Launching means a big public event.
Fact: An early launch to a small group gives faster, cheaper lessons. The big announcement can come once people keep using it.
:::

:::checklist "Ready to launch?"
- Problem written in one sentence
- 20 customer conversations documented
- At least one validation test with real payment or commitment
- One-page plan with three riskiest assumptions
- Company registered and business bank account open
- Co-founder agreement signed, if you have co-founders
- MVP that delivers the core promise
- List of first users to invite
:::

:::faq
### What is the first step in starting a startup business?
Define the problem and test it with the people who have it. Talk to potential customers about the last time they faced the problem, then run a small test that asks them to commit money or time before you build anything big.
### How do I validate a startup idea without money?
Interview potential customers, then offer a pre-order, deposit or paid pilot through a simple page or direct message. You can deliver the first version manually. If people pay, you have evidence; if not, you've saved months.
### How long does it take to launch a startup?
It depends on the idea and what the first version needs. Many founders can test demand within weeks and launch a basic version to a small group soon after. Reaching steady growth takes much longer.
### Do I need a co-founder to start a startup?
No, but it helps if your co-founder covers skills you lack, such as technical building or sales. If you go solo, plan how you'll access those skills through freelancers, partners or advisors.
### What's the difference between a startup and a small business?
A small business serves a known market in a proven way and aims for steady profit. A startup tests a new way of solving a problem and aims to grow fast, so it carries more uncertainty and usually needs more validation.
:::
