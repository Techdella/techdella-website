To launch a startup well, set up six things before launch day: clear positioning, a properly registered company with founder and customer documents, a website that explains the offer and captures interest, a plan for how your first 100 customers will find you, simple systems for payments and support, and enough money to last at least 12 months. Launch day itself is not the finish line. It's the first day of distribution.

Most founders spend months on the product and days on everything else. This guide is the "everything else" list, with country notes for the US, UK and Nigeria.

:::takeaways
- If you can't explain who it's for and why it's better in one line, fix that before anything else.
- Get the legal basics done now: company, founder agreement, terms and privacy policy.
- Your website and first acquisition channel should be live and collecting leads before launch day.
- Plan for at least 12 months of expenses. Survival is mostly about time.
:::

## Why survival takes planning

New businesses close at a steady rate in their early years. In the US, about one in five new establishments doesn't make it to its first anniversary, and about half are gone within five years.

These are all kinds of establishments, not only tech startups, but the lesson carries: the first years are the riskiest, and every month of runway buys you another chance to find what works.

## What to set up before launching a startup

:::steps "The pre-launch setup"
1. Positioning | Write one line: "For [customer] who [problem], [product] is the [category] that [key benefit], unlike [alternative]." Test it on ten target customers.
2. Company and legal | Register the company, sign a co-founder agreement with vesting, and publish terms of service and a privacy policy.
3. Brand basics | Name checked for conflicts, a simple logo and color palette, and consistent social handles. Clear beats clever.
4. Website | Headline that states the benefit, how it works, proof, pricing or "request access," and a form that captures email.
5. Acquisition plan | Pick one or two channels for the first 100 customers and a weekly target for each.
6. Operating systems | Payments, invoicing, customer support inbox, a CRM or spreadsheet, and analytics on the website.
7. Validation | Pre-sales, pilots or a waitlist with real commitment, so launch day has buyers ready.
8. Runway | Twelve months of expenses mapped, with what you'll cut if revenue is slow.
9. Trust | Testimonials from pilot users, a real address or contact details, and clear policies.
:::

## 1. Positioning: what exactly does your startup solve?

Positioning is the decision about who you serve, what you replace, and why you're better for that person. It shapes your website headline, ads, sales calls and pricing. A strong [value proposition](/glossary/value-proposition/) names the customer and the outcome, not the technology.

Weak: "An AI-powered platform for business growth."
Strong: "Shift scheduling for clinics that takes ten minutes a week instead of three hours."

## 3. A website that does a job

Your launch website has one job: turn a curious visitor into a lead or a customer. It needs a clear headline, a short "how it works," proof, a price or a clear next step, and a form. Speed and mobile layout matter, because many first visits come from social links on phones.

## 4. How exactly will customers find you?

Write the plan as numbers. Example: "Founder outreach: 50 messages a week, aiming for 10 calls and 2 pilots. Content: one problem-focused article a week. Partnerships: two integration partners by month three."

Good first channels for most startups:

- **Founder-led outreach** to a researched list of ideal customers.
- **Communities** where your customers ask questions.
- **Search** for problem-aware queries.
- **Partners** who already serve your customers.
- **Email** to your waitlist, with a simple welcome sequence. See [email marketing](/services/email-marketing/).

## 5. Runway: a worked example

The SBA's planning guide recommends counting at least one year of monthly expenses ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/calculate-your-startup-costs)). Here's the arithmetic with illustrative numbers:

- Monthly costs: founder living costs 1,500 + tools and hosting 300 + marketing 600 + contractors 1,000 = **3,400**
- Twelve months: 3,400 × 12 = **40,800**
- Expected revenue in the first year (conservative): **9,000**
- **Money you need: 40,800 − 9,000 = 31,800**

Then write the "slow revenue" plan: which costs you'd cut first (contractors, then marketing) and how many extra months that buys. If cutting contractors saves 1,000 a month, that's 31,800 ÷ 2,400 = about **13 months** instead of about 9 (31,800 ÷ 3,400).

## 6. Why should people trust this startup?

New brands have no reputation yet, so you borrow trust:

- **Pilot testimonials** with names and roles, used with permission.
- **Real contact details**, a support email that gets answered, and clear refund and privacy policies.
- **Founder visibility:** who you are, why you're building this, and where people can find you.
- **Consistent branding** across website, socials and documents. See [branding at TechDella Studios](studios:/services/branding/).

## The first 30 days after launch

Launch week usually brings a burst of attention. What you do in the next month decides whether it turns into customers.

- **Reply to everyone, fast.** Every sign-up, comment and question in the first weeks is a chance to learn and to convert.
- **Call your first users.** Ask what made them sign up, what confused them and what nearly stopped them. Write the answers down.
- **Fix the biggest friction first.** If people sign up but don't come back, improve onboarding before you add features.
- **Keep the channel running.** Send the outreach messages, publish the article and follow up with partners every week, even when launch excitement fades.
- **Review your numbers weekly.** Visitors, sign-ups, active users, paying customers and cash. Five numbers on one page are enough.

:::myth-fact
Myth: A big launch day brings a flood of lasting customers.
Fact: Launch spikes fade fast. Lasting growth comes from a channel you work every week after launch.
Myth: Marketing should start after the product is finished.
Fact: Positioning, a website and a waitlist should be live before launch, so you launch to people who are already interested.
Myth: Legal paperwork can wait until you have revenue.
Fact: Co-founder agreements, terms and privacy policies are cheapest and easiest before money and customers are involved.
:::

:::checklist "Launch-readiness checklist"
- One-line positioning tested on ten target customers
- Company registered, business bank account open
- Co-founder agreement with vesting signed
- Terms of service and privacy policy published
- Website live with clear headline, proof and lead capture
- Analytics and conversion tracking working
- Acquisition plan with weekly targets for one or two channels
- Payments, support inbox and CRM set up
- Twelve-month runway mapped with a slow-revenue plan
- Pilot testimonials collected with permission
:::

:::faq
### What is a startup launch strategy?
A launch strategy is your plan for getting the first customers: who you target, the message, the channels, and the weekly activity and numbers behind each channel. It covers the weeks before launch and, more importantly, the months after.
### Why do startups fail after launch?
Common reasons include running out of money, building something not enough people want, and unit economics that don't work. Many of these trace back to skipping validation and launching without a plan for steady customer acquisition.
### When should founders start marketing a startup?
Before launch. Positioning, a website and a waitlist should be in place weeks or months ahead, so launch day reaches people already interested. Search and content take time to build, so start them early.
### How much money do I need before launching a startup?
Work out your monthly costs, multiply by at least 12, and subtract a conservative estimate of first-year revenue. That gap is roughly what you need from savings, revenue or funding. Plan which costs you'd cut if revenue is slow.
### Do I need a website before launching?
Yes, in most cases. Customers, partners and investors check your website before they reply. A simple, clear site that explains the offer and captures leads is enough to start.
:::
