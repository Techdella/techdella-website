You get your first 100 customers by hand. Pick a narrow group of people who have your problem right now, spend time where they already talk, reach out to them personally, and make the page they land on do one job. Paul Graham called this [doing things that don't scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), and it's still how most startups get started. This 30-day plan breaks it into four weeks, with the numbers to plan around.

:::takeaways
- Your first customers are early adopters with an urgent problem, not "everyone who could use this."
- Work backwards from 100: decide how many conversations you need, then do them.
- Be useful in communities for a week before you mention your product.
- Personal outreach beats broadcast. Reference something specific about each person.
- After 30 days, keep the two channels that worked and drop the rest.
- If nobody buys after real effort, the problem is usually the product or the offer, not the marketing.
:::

## How many conversations does it take to get your first 100 customers?

Before you start, back-solve the funnel. These are **illustrative rates**: replace them with your own after week two.

| Step | Assumed rate | What you need for 100 customers |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | 100 | 100 |
| Discovery calls or demos, at 20% close rate | 100 ÷ 0.20 | 500 calls |
| Personal messages, at 10% booking a call | 500 ÷ 0.10 | 5,000 messages |

5,000 personal messages in 30 days isn't realistic for one founder. That's the point of doing the math: it tells you that you also need inbound (content, communities, referrals) and that improving each rate matters. If your close rate goes to 30% and booking rate to 15%, you need about 333 calls and 2,222 messages. Better targeting and a sharper offer do more than extra hours.

## Week 1: define and find your first customers

### Days 1–2: define your ideal first customer (not everyone)

Your first 100 customers aren't "everyone with our problem." They're early adopters who have the problem right now and are actively looking for a fix. "Small business owners" is too broad to act on. "Solo SaaS founders who launched in the last six months and are struggling with customer onboarding" is narrow enough to find.

:::checklist "Days 1–2"
- Write your ideal customer in one sentence: industry, company size, specific pain, timing
- List 10 online places these people gather: subreddits, Slack and Discord groups, LinkedIn groups, forums
- Note the exact words they use to describe the problem. This becomes your copy
- Find five real posts or comments where someone describes the problem
:::

Search tip: try `site:reddit.com "your problem"` in Google to find people describing the problem in their own words.

### Days 3–4: learn from your competitors' customers

Your competitors have already found customers. Find out where, and what those customers wish were different.

1. List five direct competitors, or the workarounds people use instead of a product like yours.
2. Check where they show up: communities, podcasts, guest posts, social accounts.
3. Read their reviews on G2, Capterra, Trustpilot or the app stores.
4. Make a complaints list. What do customers keep asking for?
5. The complaint that appears most often is your sharpest marketing angle.

### Days 5–7: join communities and be useful

Join three active communities where your customers spend time. Spend the first days reading: the culture, the recurring questions, who people listen to. Then answer a few questions a day with genuinely helpful replies. Keep a document of every pain point and question you see.

:::callout warning "Don't pitch yet"
Your only goal this week is to become a recognized, helpful member. Dropping your link on day one gets you ignored or banned, and it burns the community you'll need next week.
:::

## Week 2: create content and start direct outreach

### Days 8–9: write one piece of proof-of-value content

Pick the biggest pain point from week one and solve it completely, without needing your product. A thorough guide, a video walkthrough, or a worked example of how someone solved it. Publish it on your site, then share it helpfully in the communities you joined and on LinkedIn.

### Days 10–11: build a lead magnet

A lead magnet is something useful enough that people give you their email for it: a template you use internally, a checklist, a small calculator, a swipe file, or a five-day email course. Put it on a simple page with one form, then follow up with a short [email sequence](marketing:/services/email-marketing/).

### Days 12–14: start direct outreach

Most first customers won't find you. You find them. That means personal emails and messages to people who have the problem you solve.

**A B2B outreach template:**

> Subject: Quick question about [their specific problem]
>
> Hi [Name], I saw your post in [community] about [the problem they described]. I'm building [product] to solve exactly that for [their type of company]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call? I'd like your honest feedback on how we're approaching it. If it's not relevant, no worries at all.
>
> [Your name], [title]

Goal for the week: 20 to 30 personalized messages, 5 to 10 calls booked, and your first one to three customers or committed beta users. "Personalized" means you reference something specific about them. Spray-and-pray doesn't work.

:::myth-fact
Myth: Cold email to businesses isn't regulated.
Fact: In the US, the FTC says CAN-SPAM makes no exception for business-to-business email. Identify yourself honestly, include a way to opt out, and honor opt-outs within 10 business days.
Myth: You need a big audience before you launch.
Fact: You need a few dozen conversations with the right people. Audience comes later and grows faster once early customers talk about you.
:::

## Week 3: fix conversion before you add traffic

### Days 15–16: audit your landing page

Your [landing page](marketing:/glossary/landing-page/) has one job: get the right visitor to take one action.

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Generic headline | Use your customers' words: "[Outcome] for [specific audience] who are tired of [specific problem]" |
| No clear call to action | One primary action above the fold, impossible to miss |
| Asking for too much | Name and email only. Ask for more later |
| No proof | Add real quotes from beta users or early customers, with their permission |
| Talking about features | Talk about outcomes the customer gets, in their terms |

### Days 17–18: launch a "first 100" offer

Early adopters like being early. Give them a reason to commit now: a founding-member price locked in, a vote on the roadmap, or a support tier that won't exist later. If you show a counter ("23 of 100 founding spots left"), keep it true and update it.

### Days 19–21: turn customers into marketers

1. Ask every customer what almost stopped them signing up, and what convinced them.
2. Ask for a short testimonial, and make it easy with a few prompts.
3. Ask permission to write up their results as a case study.
4. Share their wins publicly, with permission.
5. Start a simple [referral program](marketing:/glossary/referral-marketing/), for example a credit for both sides.

If happy customers aren't referring anyone, either the product isn't good enough yet or you haven't made referring easy.

## Week 4: keep what works and drop what doesn't

### Days 22–24: analyze your channels honestly

| Channel | Customers | Hours spent | Money spent | Cost per customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Communities | | | | |
| Direct outreach | | | | |
| Content | | | | |
| Social media | | | | |

Fill it in with real numbers. Your [customer acquisition cost](marketing:/glossary/customer-acquisition-cost/) matters even now, and so does your time. If a channel brought nothing after ten honest hours, stop. Most channels won't work, and that's normal. Two working channels out of five is a good result.

### Days 25–27: turn your top two channels into a routine

- **Outreach working?** Write five message templates for different situations, build a list for next month, and block fixed outreach time in your calendar.
- **Content working?** Plan the next 30 days, batch-write several pieces, and connect them to an email nurture sequence.
- **Communities working?** Set two short daily sessions, track which posts bring messages, and get to know the moderators.

### Days 28–30: plan the next 100

1. Which three channels brought customers?
2. Which ones brought none? Drop them.
3. What can you automate, template or hand to someone else?
4. Where would budget for ads or tools speed things up?
5. Where do you need help: an assistant for outreach, a freelancer for content?

## Which channels work best for your business type

| Business type | Channels to try first | One tactic that fits |
|---|---|---|
| SaaS | Niche communities, direct email and LinkedIn outreach, Product Hunt, integration partners | Build in public: share progress, problems and lessons |
| Local business | Google Business Profile, local partnerships, neighborhood groups | Cross-promote with a complementary local business |
| DTC and ecommerce | Instagram and TikTok organic, micro-creators, niche communities | Send product to small creators in your niche |

## What if you have zero customers after 30 days?

If you've done this properly for 30 days and nobody has bought, the problem probably isn't your marketing. It's [product-market fit](marketing:/glossary/product-market-fit/). CB Insights' 2026 analysis of startup shutdowns makes the same point.

:::stats "Why VC-backed startups shut down"
43% | of shut-down startups cited poor product-market fit (failures could cite more than one reason) | CB Insights, 2026, analysis of 385 shutdowns with known reasons | https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/
70% | of shut-down startups cited running out of capital | CB Insights, 2026 | https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/
:::

Signs you have something people want:

- People understand the value the first time you explain it.
- Prospects ask about price before you bring it up.
- Customers tell others without being asked.
- People keep using it after the first try.

If none of those are true, pause the marketing. Go back to talking with potential customers about their problem, not your solution. Our guide to [product validation for startups](/product-validation-for-startups/) walks through how.

## Common mistakes when chasing your first customers

1. **Building in secret.** Nobody is going to steal your idea. Share progress publicly; it attracts early adopters.
2. **Scaling too early.** Manual outreach doesn't scale to 10,000 customers. It works fine for 100. Automate later.
3. **Spreading too thin.** Ten channels with a little effort each usually means none work. Pick two.
4. **Not asking for feedback.** Ask every customer what almost stopped them and what convinced them.
5. **Waiting for perfect.** Your product needs to solve one problem well, not every problem.

:::faq
### How long does it take to get your first 100 customers?
It varies widely by price, market and sales cycle. A low-priced consumer product can get there in weeks; a B2B product with a long sales cycle may take months. Use the 30-day plan to find which channels work, then repeat them until you reach 100.
### What is the fastest way to get your first customers?
Direct, personal outreach to people who already have the problem, found in communities and through competitors' reviews. It's slow per person but fast to learn from, and every conversation improves your message.
### Should I run paid ads to get my first 100 customers?
Usually not first. Ads amplify a message and offer that already convert. Get your first customers by hand, learn what makes them buy, then test ads with that message.
### Do I need a website to get my first customers?
You need at least one clear landing page with a single call to action and a way to book a call or sign up. A full website can wait.
### What if people like the idea but don't pay?
Interest isn't demand. Ask for a small commitment, such as a paid pilot or a pre-order. If nobody commits, revisit the problem, the audience or the offer before spending more on marketing.
:::
