Startup grants in Africa come mostly from foundations, corporate programs and development agencies, and most of them run once a year on a fixed calendar. To find them, start from official funder pages rather than social media forwards, filter hard by country, stage, sector and founder profile, and prepare a reusable application pack before the window opens. The major 2026 windows we checked ran for only six to nine weeks.

This guide lists programs we verified as active in 2026, with links to their official pages, then walks through how to qualify, apply and avoid scams.

:::callout warning "Programs and deadlines change every year"
Every program below ran a 2026 cycle, but dates, amounts and eligibility change between cycles. Always confirm the current call on the funder's official page before you start an application. We last checked these programs in October 2026.
:::

:::takeaways
- Grants are **non-dilutive**: you keep your equity, but you take on reporting and spending obligations.
- Most major programs open **once a year**. Missing a window usually means waiting a year.
- **Fit beats volume.** One well-matched application beats ten generic ones.
- Real funders **never** charge you to apply or guarantee that you'll win.
:::

## What are startup grants?

A startup grant is money, a prize or a support package given to a founder or company without taking equity and, normally, without repayment. Many African programs combine cash with training, mentorship and network access. In return, funders expect you to use the money for the approved purpose, report on progress and, often, join a training program or demo day.

Grants are one tool among several. For how they fit alongside angels, seed funds and debt, see our guide to [startup funding stages for African founders](/startup-funding-stages-for-african-founders/).

## How to find startup grants in Africa, step by step

:::steps
1. Decide what kind of money fits your stage | Idea-stage founders should look at founder programs and competitions. Founders with traction can target prizes and corporate programs that ask for revenue or users. Impact ventures should add foundation and development-agency programs.
2. Start from official sources | Funder websites, government innovation agencies, development finance institutions, embassies, corporate programs and university entrepreneurship centers. Use aggregator lists only to discover programs, then click through to the funder's own page.
3. Search narrowly | Combine country, sector and founder profile: "agritech grant Kenya," "women founders grant Nigeria," "climate innovation prize sub-Saharan Africa." Broad searches surface stale lists.
4. Filter on eligibility in five minutes | Check country, age, company registration, stage, sector, revenue or traction requirements, team size and reporting duties before you read anything else. Skip anything you don't clearly fit.
5. Build a reusable application pack | Prepare your core answers and documents once (checklist below), then tailor each application to the funder's goals.
6. Track every opportunity | Keep a simple sheet: program, official link, eligibility, amount, open and close dates, documents needed, status, result and feedback.
7. Apply early and follow up | Submit days before the deadline, keep a copy of your answers and record any feedback for the next cycle.
:::

### Check eligibility before you write a word

Most rejected applications fail on fit, not quality. Before you start, confirm:

- Your country and, if relevant, your citizenship and residence
- Founder age and gender criteria
- Whether you must be a registered company, and for how long
- Required stage: idea, prototype, MVP with users, or revenue
- Sector or impact focus
- What reporting the funder expects after an award

If a prize requires a working MVP with traction and you're still at the idea stage, prepare for next year's cycle instead. Our guide on [how to develop an MVP](/how-to-develop-an-mvp-for-startup/) can get you there.

## How to write a grant application reviewers can say yes to

### Lead with the problem, specifically

A weak problem statement: "Many businesses need better technology." A strong one: "Small retailers in Lagos track stock by hand, so they miss sales, restock badly and can't show the sales records lenders ask for." It names who, where, what it costs and why money would help.

### Show traction, even if it's small

Reviewers fund believable teams with evidence. Useful proof includes paying customers, pilot users, letters of intent, partnerships, retention, waitlist conversions, field research and prototype usage. If you're early, [validation evidence](/product-validation-for-startups/) such as interview findings and landing-page results counts.

### Make the use of funds concrete

"We'll use the funds for growth" tells a reviewer nothing. Tie each line to an outcome. For example (illustrative): 40% to finish MVP version 1, 25% to run a 90-day pilot with 3 partner schools, 20% for field operations, and 15% for monitoring, reporting and legal compliance. Then state what you'll have at the end: "a tested product used weekly by 200 students."

### Tailor the angle to the funder

The same startup tells a different true story to different funders. A climate prize wants environmental impact. A women-founder program wants to understand founder representation and inclusion. An engineering prize wants technical novelty and a path to commercialization. A youth program may care about jobs created.

:::myth-fact
Myth: Grants are free money with no strings attached.
Fact: Grants don't take equity, but most require reporting, milestones, spending on the approved purpose and often participation in training. Plan for that time.
Myth: The more grants you apply for, the better your chances.
Fact: Reviewers can spot generic applications quickly. A few well-matched, tailored applications usually beat a scattergun approach.
:::

:::quote "Joanna Okedara-Kalu" "Founder & CEO, TechDella"
Grant applications and sales pages often fail for the same reason: they describe the founder's idea instead of the reader's priorities. Before you write a word, read the funder's criteria and the profiles of past winners, then decide which true part of your story matches them. Write the problem the way you'd write a landing page headline: specific, local and easy to picture.
:::

## How to spot grant scams

Fake "grants" circulate widely on messaging apps and social media. Walk away if you see any of these:

- A fee to apply, "process" or "release" the grant
- No official website, or a site that doesn't match the organization's real domain
- No named organization, no deadline or no eligibility criteria
- Registration only through WhatsApp or a personal email address
- Promises that you're guaranteed to win
- Requests for banking passwords, PINs or ID documents before any selection

When in doubt, find the funder's official website independently (not through the link you were sent) and check that the call appears there.

## Your grant application pack

:::checklist "Prepare once, tailor every time"
- Company registration details (or a note on your registration plans)
- Founder and team bios, 100 words each
- One-sentence problem statement and one-paragraph solution summary
- Target customer and market, with sources
- Business model and pricing
- Traction or validation evidence
- Use of funds tied to outcomes, with a simple budget
- Impact metrics you can actually measure
- Pitch deck (10–12 slides) and a 2-minute demo video or screenshots
- A clean website or landing page reviewers can visit
:::

## Common grant application mistakes

- Applying without checking eligibility
- Vague answers that could describe any startup
- Numbers that don't add up between the budget and the narrative
- Impact claims with no way to measure them
- An outdated deck or broken website link
- Waiting until deadline day and fighting a crashed portal

## Final check before you submit

:::checklist "Before you press submit"
- I meet every eligibility criterion
- I'm applying through the official link
- Every question is answered directly
- My budget matches my narrative
- My traction and impact numbers are accurate and sourced
- My documents are the right versions and open correctly
- I've saved a copy of my answers
:::

:::faq
### Where can African startups find legitimate grants?
Start with funders' official websites: foundations such as the Tony Elumelu Foundation, corporate programs such as the Orange Social Venture Prize, and institutions such as the Royal Academy of Engineering's Africa Prize. Use aggregator lists to discover programs, but always apply through the funder's own page.
### Are startup grants in Africa free money?
They don't take equity and usually don't need repaying, but they aren't free of obligations. Expect to report on progress, spend on the approved purpose and often take part in training or mentoring.
### When do most African grant programs open?
Each program sets its own calendar. In 2026, the Tony Elumelu Foundation ran from January to March, Orange's prize closed in May and the Africa Prize ran from July to September. Check each official page and set reminders for the next cycle.
### Can idea-stage founders get grants?
Some programs accept idea-stage founders, especially founder training programs and youth competitions. Others require a working MVP, users or revenue; for example, the Africa Prize asks software entries for a functional MVP and user traction.
### How do I know if a grant is a scam?
Real funders don't charge application fees, guarantee awards or ask for banking passwords. Check that the call appears on the organization's official website, reached independently, with clear eligibility, deadlines and contact details.
:::
