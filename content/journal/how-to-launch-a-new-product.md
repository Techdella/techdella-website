To launch a new product, work in this order: define who it's for and what changes for them, validate that they'll pay before you build everything, grow a small pre-launch audience, choose two or three channels your buyers already use, launch everything in one coordinated week, then use the data to improve. Most launches fail on the first two steps, not on launch day.

:::takeaways
- Validate demand with real commitments (signups, pre-orders, paid pilots) before you spend on launch.
- A small, engaged waitlist beats a large cold one.
- Pick two or three channels and do them well, rather than being everywhere.
- Prepare every asset before launch week: landing page, demo, emails, ads, support answers, tracking.
- Judge the launch on signups, purchases and retention, not impressions.
- Plan the four weeks after launch as carefully as launch day.
:::

## Why product launches fail

Most launches don't fail because the idea was bad. They fail because nobody checked whether enough people wanted it, or because the money ran out before the team found out.

A structured launch protects you from both. It forces validation before spending, and it stops you burning budget on channels that don't work.

## Step 1: start with a clear product vision

Answer four questions in plain language before you write any marketing:

- What problem does this solve, and for whom, specifically?
- What does the customer's day look like before and after using it?
- Why would they choose it over what they do today (including doing nothing)?
- What single result should they get in their first week?

Then write one sentence: "We help [specific customer] [get specific result] without [current pain]." If that sentence makes a potential buyer say "I need that," it becomes the base for your landing page, ads and emails.

## Step 2: know your target audience

Research what people actually think and say, not what you assume:

- **Communities.** Read the forums, subreddits, Slack groups and LinkedIn threads where your buyers ask for help. Note the problems they repeat and the exact words they use.
- **Interviews.** One 30-minute conversation about someone's real workflow teaches you more than dozens of checkbox survey answers. Aim for 10 to 15.
- **Reviews of competitors.** Complaints in G2, Capterra or app store reviews show the gap you can fill.
- **Search data.** Keyword tools show which questions people type, and those questions become your launch content.

Turn what you learn into one or two short buyer profiles: role, situation, trigger for buying, objections, and where they spend time online.

## Step 3: validate before you build everything

Validation means proving people will commit before you invest fully. You don't need a finished product, just a clear enough offer to test. Our guide to [product validation for startups](/product-validation-for-startups/) goes deeper.

| Validation method | What it tests | How strong the signal is |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page with a waitlist | Interest in the promise | Weak to medium: an email costs nothing |
| Fake-door test (ads to a "coming soon" page) | Whether the message gets clicks and signups | Medium, if you're honest on the page that it's coming soon |
| Interviews and surveys | Whether the problem is painful and frequent | Medium: people are kind in interviews |
| Pre-orders, deposits or paid pilots | Real buying intent | Strong: money is the clearest signal |

:::myth-fact
Myth: A big waitlist proves demand.
Fact: Signups are cheap. Pre-orders, deposits and paid pilots prove demand. Ask for a small commitment before you build everything.
Myth: Launch day is the most important day.
Fact: Validation and the four weeks after launch decide most outcomes. Launch day is when you find out whether the earlier work was right.
:::

## Step 4: plan your launch in six weeks

A six-week plan is enough for most small launches. Stretch it if your product is complex or your market is regulated.

| Week | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audience and positioning | Buyer profiles, positioning statement, key messages |
| 2 | Brand assets and landing page | Launch page, email templates, visuals, demo video script |
| 3 | Campaign planning | Ad copy and creative, launch offer, content plan, press and partner list |
| 4 | Launch week | Paid and organic campaigns live, emails sent, partner and press outreach |
| 5 | Optimize | Retargeting, fix drop-off points, act on feedback |
| 6 | Scale and sustain | Tracking review, post-launch roadmap, keep what works |

You can run this solo by breaking each week into daily tasks, or bring in help for the parts you're weakest at.

## Step 5: build a pre-launch audience

Your launch goes better if people are waiting for it.

- **Waitlist page.** One clear promise, one form, and a reason to join early, such as early access or a founding-member price. If you show how many people have joined, use the real number.
- **Behind-the-scenes content.** Short posts and videos showing the problem, the build, and early users. Each one points to the waitlist.
- **Partners.** Complementary products, newsletters and community leaders who already serve your buyers. Offer them something useful for their audience.
- **Beta group.** A small group who test the product, give feedback and become your first advocates.

Keep the list warm with a short email every week or two. Engagement matters more than size: a few hundred people who've followed the journey will act on launch day; thousands of cold signups mostly won't.

## Step 6: pick the right launch channels

Choose two or three channels where your buyers already spend time, and do them properly.

| Channel | Why it works | Launch tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Email | Direct line to people who asked to hear from you | A short pre-launch sequence, a launch announcement, a last-chance reminder |
| Social media | Storytelling and community | Founder posts, demos, behind-the-scenes, customer reactions |
| Paid ads | Fast reach and testing | Retarget site visitors and waitlist; test two or three messages |
| PR and creators | Credibility from a trusted voice | Press kit, founder story, early access for relevant creators |
| Launch platforms (Product Hunt, Hacker News, niche directories) | Early adopters for software and digital tools | A clear launch post, a founder comment, fast replies |
| Search | Demand that keeps coming after launch week | Pages for the problems you solve and the comparisons buyers make |

## Step 7: create your launch content

Prepare these before launch week:

1. **Launch landing page.** Headline in the customer's words, the before-and-after, proof, one call to action, and answers to the top objections. If you need one built, see [website design at Studios](studios:/services/website-design/).
2. **Demo video.** Start with the problem, show the product solving it in the simplest way, end with the result. Not a tour of every button.
3. **Email sequence.** Pre-launch (the problem), launch day (the solution and offer), follow-up (proof and answers), last call (deadline, if there's a real one). Our [email marketing service](marketing:/services/email-marketing/) can set this up.
4. **Founder story.** Why you built it, what you learned, who it's for. Use it on social, in press pitches and on your about page.
5. **Press kit.** A one-paragraph description, key facts, screenshots, logo files and a contact.
6. **Support answers.** Written answers to the ten questions you expect most.

A simple rule for every piece: educate (help them see the problem clearly), excite (show the better outcome), engage (give one clear next step).

## Step 8: run launch week

:::checklist "Launch week checklist"
- Landing page and site are live and tested on mobile
- Analytics and conversion tracking are firing (test a signup and a purchase yourself)
- Launch emails are scheduled and tested
- Ads are approved and live, with daily budgets set
- Social posts and the demo video are ready to publish
- Partners and early users know when to share
- Support is staffed and answers are ready
- A feedback form or short survey is live
- Someone owns a daily review of the numbers
:::

During the week:

- **Host a live demo** or launch call so people can ask questions in real time. Record it.
- **Reward early adopters** with a real, time-limited offer, and honor the terms you announce.
- **Ask your network to share early.** Engagement in the first hours helps posts reach more people.
- **Stay human.** Reply to comments personally, thank people who share, address problems openly, show up on camera.

Watch the funnel daily. If traffic is high but signups are low, it's a message or offer problem. If one source converts far better than others, move budget there.

:::quote "Joanna Okedara-Kalu" "Founder & CEO, TechDella"
During launch week, I'd rather have one person reviewing the numbers every morning than a perfect content calendar. Launches rarely go to plan. A message that tested well can fall flat, and a channel you almost skipped can carry the week. Decide in advance what you'll change if signups lag, so you're adjusting from data, not from panic.
:::

## Step 9: measure and improve after launch

The launch is the start of the feedback loop. Track the numbers that tie to revenue:

| Metric | What it tells you | What to do if it's weak |
|---|---|---|
| Conversion rate (visit to signup or purchase) | Whether the page and offer work | Rewrite the headline and offer in customers' words; cut form fields |
| Customer acquisition cost by channel | Which channels are worth scaling | Cut channels that cost more than a customer is worth |
| Activation (first key action) | Whether new users reach the first result | Simplify onboarding; add a welcome sequence |
| Retention (weeks 1, 4 and 12) | Whether the product delivers lasting value | Talk to people who left; fix the top reason |
| Revenue and payback | Whether the launch pays for itself | Revisit pricing, offer and channel mix |

Then improve, in this order:

1. **Talk to early customers.** Ask what almost stopped them, what convinced them, and what they'd change.
2. **Use their words.** Update headlines, ads and emails with the phrases customers use.
3. **Test one thing at a time.** Headline, offer, call to action or audience. Give each test enough traffic to be meaningful.
4. **Double down on what works.** Move budget and time from weak channels to strong ones.
5. **Start search early.** Pages that answer your buyers' questions keep bringing visitors after launch week ends. See our [SEO service](marketing:/services/seo/).

## Common mistakes when you launch a new product

1. **Skipping validation.** Building for months, then launching to silence.
2. **Being everywhere.** Five half-done channels instead of two done well.
3. **Launching without tracking.** If you can't see which channel brought customers, you can't improve.
4. **Fake urgency.** Countdowns that reset and fake scarcity damage trust fast.
5. **Stopping after launch week.** Most of the learning, and often most of the revenue, comes in the weeks after.
6. **Ignoring support.** Slow answers during launch turn interested people into critics.

:::faq
### How long does it take to launch a new product?
For a small product with a validated idea, six weeks of focused launch work is realistic: positioning, assets, campaign planning, launch week, then two weeks of optimization. Building the product itself comes before that and depends on scope.
### What is the first step in launching a new product?
Get specific about who it's for and what problem it solves, then validate demand with real commitments such as pre-orders or paid pilots. Marketing works much better once you know people want it.
### How do I build an audience before launch?
Create a waitlist page with a clear promise, share behind-the-scenes progress, partner with people who already reach your buyers, and run a small beta. Email the list regularly so it stays warm.
### Which channels are best for a product launch?
The ones your buyers already use. Email is almost always one of them. Add one or two others, such as LinkedIn for B2B, Instagram or TikTok for consumers, or launch platforms for software tools.
### What should I measure after a product launch?
Conversion rate, acquisition cost by channel, activation, retention and revenue. Impressions and likes are useful context, but they don't tell you whether the launch is working.
:::
